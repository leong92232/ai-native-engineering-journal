# Let the System Grade Itself

I wrote before about forcing every decision in my investment system to carry its context — what triggered it, my mood, my confidence, recorded at the moment it happen.

But recording, I learned, is only half a loop. A decision with context and no outcome is just a diary entry. Somebody still has to come back later and answer the question that actually matter: so — was it right?

And here's the honest part: I never came back. Not reliably.

Checking your own old decisions is tedious, and worse, it threaten the ego. Memory does the editing for you — the wins stay vivid, the losses get a story attached ("market was crazy that week"), and the ones that quietly went nowhere just fade. Ask me how my trading going and I'd give you a feeling. A warm, self-authored, statistically worthless feeling.

So I stopped trusting myself to close the loop, and made the system do it.

The rule is dumb on purpose: any position still sitting there after thirty days get graded automatically. Win, loss, or stopped out — judged against the criteria I set when I made the trade, not the criteria I'd prefer in hindsight. No ceremony, no waiting for me to feel ready. The clock decide when grading happen; the recorded rules decide the grade.

And then — this part matter more than I expected — the report come to me. Once a month, the summary arrive in my chat, invited or not. Because a dashboard I have to visit is a report card I can avoid, and I know exactly how disciplined I am about voluntarily reading my own report card.

The first few reports were uncomfortable in a very specific way: the numbers disagreed with my self-image. Triggers I was proud of graded mediocre. My "high confidence" turned out to mean almost nothing. One pattern I barely respected was quietly my best performer. None of this was visible in the feeling. All of it was sitting in the filed outcomes.

I think this generalize way past trading. Every team make graded-able decisions constantly — forecasts, hires, bets on what to build next. Almost every team record the decision. Almost none go back and grade it, for the exact same reasons I didn't: tedious, and the ego is at stake. So the track record never form, and next quarter's decisions get made on the same warm feelings.

Recording tell you what you decided.

Grading tell you who you are as a decider. And that's precisely the report nobody volunteer to read — so build a system that doesn't ask.
