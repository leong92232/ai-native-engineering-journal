# The Result Is the Deliverable, Not the Idea

I like the term forward-deployed engineer. Sit close to the business, skip the layers, ship the thing.

But I notice something when I sit with it long enough. The appeal of the term is doing a lot of work that the actual practice has to earn separately.

Because "close to the business" can mean two very different things.

One version: I understand what the business actually need, I go build the right thing, fast, because I removed the distance between the idea and the delivery.

Other version: I'm just fast, and close, and nobody upstream is checking whether what I built should exist.

Same title. Very different outcome.

I used to think the idea was the hard part. Spend the meeting, find the insight, name the pattern, feel smart about it. Then hand it off, let someone else worry about whether it ship.

That's backward. The idea is cheap now. Everyone in the room can generate a decent idea, AI can generate ten more in the time it take to finish your coffee. What's not cheap — what was never cheap — is whether the thing actually land in the business's hand, working, and change something.

Result is the deliverable. Not the idea.

This sound obvious when I write it out. But I catch myself, and I catch other people, measuring the wrong unit constantly. We say "I proposed X." We say "I identified the root cause." We say "I designed a nice architecture for it." All true, all useless, if X never ship, if the root cause fix never got deployed, if the nice architecture stay a diagram.

So what actually make forward-deployed work, not just sound good?

I think it's this: the person still need to carry judgment with them into the business, not just speed.

Speed without judgment just mean you ship the wrong thing faster, and now it's in production, and now someone has to notice it's wrong, and now someone has to unwind it. That's not "close to the business." That's a mess with a shorter fuse.

Judgment is knowing which request is actually the problem, and which request is a symptom of a problem the person asking doesn't see yet. Judgment is knowing when the fast path is fine, and when it's going to cost you in six months. Judgment is the same boundary-thinking I keep writing about in every other entry here — it doesn't disappear just because you're standing physically or organizationally closer to the business. If anything it matters more, because there's no layer left to catch you.

So the shape I hold now:

Get close to the business. Move fast. But carry the judgment with you, or "close" just means unaccountable.

The result is the deliverable.

The idea was never the hard part.
